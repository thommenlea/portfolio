Teaching future teachers to plan for competence, with AI as a sparring partner

I redesigned and taught a 13-week course in which pre-service teachers plan a complete teaching unit, from a curriculum competency to an eight-lesson plan with assessment. In 2026 I added a strand on generative AI that trains one skill, judging what the AI produces.

How I worked

My process, in ADDIE terms

Corporate L&D teams often plan in the five ADDIE phases. Here is where each part of the course sits.

  1. Ainsights

    Analyze

    A week-1 needs survey on AI use and confidence, plus a SOFT self-assessment of each learner’s planning skills.

  2. Darchitecture

    Design

    Backward design from competency to objectives, assessment and tasks, with a 10-criterion rubric tied to every graded step.

  3. Dedit_note

    Develop

    A OneNote workbook built on one four-part chapter template, slide decks with timed facilitator notes, and a custom Gemini Gem.

  4. Ico_present

    Implement

    Seven workshops alternating with self-study, built around each learner’s own unit and regular peer feedback.

  5. Emonitoring

    Evaluate

    A rubric-scored final paper, a start-and-end survey and anonymous course feedback.

The challenge

What made this course hard to design

link

A long chain of decisions

Learners plan a full unit the way the Swiss curriculum, Lehrplan 21, expects: start from a competency, work out what pupils need to know and do, write objectives, plan assessment, then design tasks. Each step depends on the one before.

schedule

Mostly self-directed

Only about 12 of the 75 hours happen in workshops. The workbook has to carry learners through the rest on their own.

smart_toy

AI already in use

In the week-1 survey, 10 of 11 respondents already used ChatGPT, and 9 had used AI for subject research. A vague prompt returns a plan that looks finished, so judging AI output had to become a learning goal.

Translated for corporate L&D

The planning process follows backward-design logic, with outcomes first, assessment second and learning activities last. Each step maps to a term a corporate L&D team would use.

What I designed

Five design decisions

account_tree

One process, one workbook, one rubric

The planning process is the spine of the course. Each step is a workbook chapter, the topic of an in-person session and a criterion in the final rubric, so learners always know which part of their paper they are building. Steps that mainly support thinking are discussed in class but not written up, which keeps the paper focused on decisions.

menu_book

Every chapter has the same four parts

I turned the course’s PDF planning guide into a OneNote workbook that learners copy into their own notebooks and write in directly. Every chapter follows the same four-part pattern, so learners spend their effort on the content instead of on finding their way around.

date_range

A blended rhythm around each learner’s own unit

In-person weeks alternate with self-study weeks. Every workshop follows the same arc: a short retrieval or progress check, a brief input, time to apply it to the learner’s own unit, and peer feedback. My facilitator notes time each block to the minute and mark optional “booster” slides for groups that move faster.

Activities kept learners talking about their own work: speed dating to compare competency analyses, a two-minute elevator pitch for the rationale, a matching puzzle on the five task types in online breakout rooms, and a SOFT self-assessment (a SWOT variant for one’s own skills) in week 1 that learners revisited in the final session.

psychology

An AI strand that trains judgment

AI runs through the semester as a thread. Each activity sits at the planning step where it helps, and the activities keep coming back to checking the output against the course’s quality criteria.

  1. quiz
    Week 1

    Needs survey

    A five-minute anonymous survey, organized by ADDIE phase, asked about tools, confidence and what learners wanted to learn.

  2. chat
    Early weeks

    The vague-prompt experiment

    Pairs ran a deliberately vague prompt (“Create a lesson plan for two lessons on the production of goods and services”) and rated the result against six features of competency-oriented teaching (Feindt and Meyer, 2010). They then compared it with a context-rich prompt and with a master prompt that sets a role and rules, such as always producing a standard and a scaffolded version of each worksheet for pupils who need language support.

  3. travel_explore
    Content analysis

    Source-grounded research

    Literature search with Consensus, a two-step Deep Research workflow in Gemini, and NotebookLM loaded with the learner’s own sources, introduced with a live demo notebook.

  4. smart_toy
    Task design

    A custom planning assistant

    A master prompt and a Gemini Gem that I built on the curriculum and the LUKAS task model, introduced with a plain warning that its output comes with no guarantee and needs checking.

  5. tune
    Differentiation

    Adapting tasks

    Learners asked AI for ideas to adapt one of their own tasks for a different level or language demand, chose one, and got peer feedback on the result.

  6. format_quote
    Throughout

    Disclosure

    AI use must be cited in the paper, and correct citation is part of the rubric.

  7. assignment_turned_in
    Final session

    End survey and course feedback

    Follow-up confidence questions, plus questions on how learners actually used AI in their units.

checklist

Assessment that mirrors the process

The final paper is an individual unit plan of 8 to 12 pages for about eight lessons, built on one of two curriculum competencies. An analytic rubric scores ten criteria from 0 to 3 points. The task set counts double because every earlier decision feeds into it.

Learners could hand in their content analysis, objectives and task-set overview early for feedback. The final session is a mandatory “highlights” presentation, where peers give feedback in three moves: feed up (what is the goal?), feed back (where are you now?) and feed forward (what comes next?).

Results

What changed

All learners passed the final unit plan, scored against the 33-point rubric. I also collected three anonymous measures: the week-1 survey (11 responses), an end-of-semester survey in the final session (6 responses) and a course feedback form (7 responses). Together they cover Kirkpatrick Level 1 (reaction) and Level 2 (learning), assessed through the rubric and self-reported in the surveys.

insightsThese are self-ratings, not test scores. The two groups are not matched person by person, the end survey reached about half the group, and the item wording changed slightly between surveys. Read the shift as a direction, not a measured effect.

3.2 of 5

Average rating for how much AI improved the quality of their final unit, where 1 means no improvement.

4 of 6

Named ChatGPT among their most valuable tools; 2 named NotebookLM.

format_quote

In open answers, learners said prompt quality changes the result, AI does better with vetted sources than with its own research, and a plan needs repeated human revision before it fits. One respondent said the course mostly consolidated existing skills, and another found AI very unreliable.

Reflection

Looking back

tuneWhat I’d change next time
