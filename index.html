Case study · Blended course redesign · Spring 2026

Teaching future teachers to plan for competence, with AI as a sparring partner

I redesigned and taught a 13-week course in which pre-service teachers plan a complete teaching unit, from a curriculum competency to an eight-lesson plan with assessment. In 2026 I added a strand on generative AI that trains one skill, judging what the AI produces.

Role
Design lead and instructor. I redesigned the course, wrote most of the materials and taught the Brugg section. A faculty colleague co-authored the workbook as subject-matter expert.
Learners
About 12 pre-service teachers for lower secondary school (grades 7–9) in Economics, Work and Household, a subject close to Family and Consumer Sciences in the U.S.
Format
Blended. Seven 105-minute workshops (one online), four self-study weeks (two with optional coaching), and a self-paced OneNote workbook.
Scope
13 weeks, March to June 2026. 3 ECTS credits, about 75 hours of learner time.
Assessment
An individual unit plan of 8–12 pages covering about eight lessons, scored with a 10-criterion analytic rubric.
Outcome
All learners passed the final unit plan.
Tools
OneNote, Moodle, Mentimeter, Google Forms, ChatGPT, Gemini (Deep Research and a custom Gem), NotebookLM, Consensus.
Institution
FHNW School of Education, Switzerland. Course: Fachdidaktik WAH 2.2, “Kompetenzorientiert planen” (Planning for competence).

The brief

Pre-service teachers in this module plan a complete teaching unit the way the Swiss curriculum, Lehrplan 21, expects. They start from a competency, work out what pupils need to know and be able to do, write observable objectives, decide how to assess them, and only then design the learning tasks. It is a long chain of decisions, and each one depends on the one before.

Only about 12 of the 75 hours happen in workshops. The workbook has to carry learners through the rest on their own.

The 2026 group also arrived using AI. In the week-1 survey, 10 of 11 respondents already used ChatGPT, and 9 had used AI for the subject-research step of planning. A vague prompt returns a plan that looks finished. Judging whether it meets the course’s quality criteria takes the same expertise the course is meant to build, so the AI strand had to train that judgment.

Translated for corporate L&D

The planning process follows backward-design logic, with outcomes first, assessment second and learning activities last. The table maps each step to the term a corporate L&D team would use.

What I designed

One process, one workbook, one rubric

The planning process is the spine of the course. Each step is a workbook chapter, the topic of an in-person session and a criterion in the final rubric, so learners always know which part of their paper they are building. Steps that mainly support thinking are discussed in class but not written up, which keeps the paper focused on decisions.

Every chapter has the same four parts

I turned the course’s planning guide into a OneNote workbook that learners copy into their own notebooks and write in directly. Every chapter follows the same four-part pattern, so learners spend their effort on the content instead of on finding their way around.

A blended rhythm around each learner’s own unit

In-person weeks alternate with self-study weeks. Every workshop follows the same arc: a short retrieval or progress check, a brief input, time to apply it to the learner’s own unit, and peer feedback. My facilitator notes time each block to the minute and mark optional “booster” slides for groups that move faster.

Activities kept learners talking about their own work: speed dating to compare competency analyses, a two-minute elevator pitch for the rationale, a matching puzzle on the five task types in online breakout rooms, and a SOFT self-assessment (a SWOT variant for one’s own skills) in week 1 that learners revisited in the final session.

An AI strand that trains judgment

AI runs through the semester as a thread. Each activity sits at the planning step where it helps, and the activities keep coming back to checking the output against the course’s quality criteria.

  1. Week 1

    Needs survey

    A five-minute anonymous survey, organized by ADDIE phase, asked about tools, confidence and what learners wanted to learn.

  2. Early weeks

    The vague-prompt experiment

    Pairs ran a deliberately vague prompt (“Create a lesson plan for two lessons on the production of goods and services”) and rated the result against six features of competency-oriented teaching (Feindt and Meyer, 2010). They then compared it with a context-rich prompt and with a master prompt that sets a role and rules, such as always producing a standard and a scaffolded version of each worksheet for pupils who need language support.

  3. Content analysis

    Source-grounded research

    Literature search with Consensus, a two-step Deep Research workflow in Gemini, and NotebookLM loaded with the learner’s own sources, introduced with a live demo notebook.

  4. Task design

    A custom planning assistant

    A master prompt and a Gemini Gem that I built on the curriculum and the LUKAS task model, introduced with a plain warning that its output comes with no guarantee and needs checking.

  5. Differentiation

    Adapting tasks

    Learners asked AI for ideas to adapt one of their own tasks for a different level or language demand, chose one, and got peer feedback on the result.

  6. Throughout

    Disclosure

    AI use must be cited in the paper, and correct citation is part of the rubric.

  7. Final session

    End survey and course feedback

    Follow-up confidence questions, plus questions on how learners actually used AI in their units.

Assessment that mirrors the process

The final paper is an individual unit plan of 8 to 12 pages for about eight lessons, built on one of two curriculum competencies. An analytic rubric scores ten criteria from 0 to 3 points. The task set counts double because every earlier decision feeds into it.

Learners could hand in their content analysis, objectives and task-set overview early for feedback. The final session is a mandatory “highlights” presentation, where peers give feedback in three moves: feed up (what is the goal?), feed back (where are you now?) and feed forward (what comes next?).

Results

All learners passed the final unit plan, scored against the 33-point rubric. I also collected three anonymous measures: the week-1 survey (11 responses), an end-of-semester survey in the final session (6 responses) and a course feedback form (7 responses). Together they cover Kirkpatrick Level 1 (reaction) and Level 2 (learning), assessed through the rubric and self-reported in the surveys.

These are self-ratings, not test scores. The two groups are not matched person by person, the end survey reached about half the group, and the item wording changed slightly between surveys. Read the shift as a direction, not a measured effect.

6 of 6

end-survey respondents had to correct or adapt AI suggestions at least sometimes to meet subject-teaching standards. No one chose 1 or 2 on a 5-point scale where 1 means the AI never needed correcting.

3.2 of 5

average rating for how much AI improved the quality of their final unit, where 1 means no improvement.

4 of 6

named ChatGPT among their most valuable tools; 2 named NotebookLM.

In open answers, learners described three lessons: prompt quality changes the result, AI does better with vetted sources than with its own research, and a plan needs repeated human revision before it fits. One respondent said the course mostly consolidated existing skills, and another found AI very unreliable.

Course feedback: what worked

  • A clear structure, with the final paper built step by step in the sessions
  • A paper learners can use in their own teaching
  • The mix of in-person sessions and self-study
  • Exchange with peers and the instructor

Course feedback: what to fix

  • Some sessions packed in too much for the time
  • OneNote felt clumsy to some (a Word document was allowed)
  • The expected length of each chapter was unclear
  • One self-directed learner got little extra from attending
  • Learners wanted to choose their own topic

Course feedback: 7 anonymous responses, summarized and translated.

What I’d change next time

  • Measure so the data can answer the question. Use identical wording and scale direction in both surveys, and a better way to match responses. Self-made codes linked only 1 of the 6 end-of-semester responses to a week-1 response.
  • Move more input into the workbook or short videos, so workshop time goes to practice and feedback.
  • State the expected length of each written chapter.
  • Test open topic choice. Let learners pick any curriculum competency while keeping the same rubric.

What this shows

  • Backward design from a competency to objectives, assessment and a practice sequence
  • An analytic rubric aligned with every step of the learning process
  • Blended design that pairs a self-paced workbook with facilitated workshops and minute-by-minute facilitator notes
  • AI enablement for professionals, from a needs survey and hands-on practice with source-grounded tools to a custom assistant and evaluation
  • Evaluation at Kirkpatrick Levels 1 and 2, reported with its limits
